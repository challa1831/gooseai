The Field Guide

Apple App Store rejections, in plain English.

All 27 subsections of Apple's App Store Review Guidelines, decoded — sub-rules, the rejection triggers that actually fire under each, and the fix to ship before your next submission.

1Safety

7 guidelines
Guideline 1.1

Objectionable Content

Apps with offensive, defamatory, mean-spirited content — or that capitalise on tragedy — get pulled. GenAI output counts as content.

User-Generated Content

Any app where users post text, photos or video must ship four specific safety controls — or be rejected on sight.

Kids

Apps in the Kids Category — or that target children — face the strictest data, ad and analytics rules on the store.

Read the breakdownGuideline 1.4

Physical Harm

Apps that could cause physical injury — through bad data, drug content, weapons or risky behaviour — get extra scrutiny.

Read the breakdownGuideline 1.5

Developer Information

Users must be able to easily contact you for support, and your business identity on the store must be truthful.

Data Security

Apps must implement industry-standard protection of user data; live vulnerabilities are grounds for immediate removal.

Mischaracterization

Don't report a competitor's app as objectionable when it isn't — and use law enforcement (not App Review) for actual crimes.

2Performance

5 guidelines
Guideline 2.1

App Completeness

Submit the final, on-device-tested build. No placeholders, no broken links, no 'coming soon' screens.

Read the breakdownGuideline 2.2

Beta Testing

Demos, betas and trial builds belong in TestFlight, not on the App Store proper.

Read the breakdownGuideline 2.3

Accurate Metadata

The single most common rejection category. Screenshots, title, subtitle, keywords, age rating and “What's New” must all reflect reality.

Read the breakdownGuideline 2.4

Hardware Compatibility

Apps must run on supported hardware, must not drain power or generate heat, and must work with Apple's standard inputs.

Software Requirements

Use only Apple-approved APIs, ship on the current SDK, and don't load executable code at runtime.

4Design

7 guidelines
Guideline 4.1

Copycats

Don't clone another developer's icon, name, UI or trade dress. Apple has aggressively pulled clone apps since the 2025 enforcement update.

Read the breakdownGuideline 4.2

Minimum Functionality

Apps must do something useful, unique and lasting. Repackaged websites, single-feature gimmicks and AI wrappers without value-add are removed.

Read the breakdownGuideline 4.3

Spam

Apple limits saturated categories and bans submitting multiple near-identical apps.

Read the breakdownGuideline 4.4

Extensions

Keyboards, share extensions, widgets, App Clips, Stickers and Watch extensions each have specific rules.

Read the breakdownGuideline 4.5

Apple Sites and Services

Don't scrape Apple's storefronts, mimic Apple's UI, or use Apple Music / Maps / Push in ways that violate the API terms.

Read the breakdownGuideline 4.7

Mini Apps, Mini Games, Chatbots, Plug-ins, Emulators

Hosting third-party software — emulators, mini-game launchers, AI chatbot plugins — only under tightly defined conditions.

Read the breakdownGuideline 4.8

Login Services

If you offer third-party sign-in and target consumers, you must offer Sign in with Apple — or an equivalent privacy-preserving option.

6 guidelines
Guideline 5.1

Privacy

The most-cited rejection reason in 2026. Privacy labels, ATT, account deletion and data minimisation all live here.

Read the breakdownGuideline 5.2

Intellectual Property

You must own or license everything — code, music, characters, trademarks, public-figure likenesses, and Apple-trademarked terms.

Read the breakdownGuideline 5.3

Gaming, Gambling, and Lotteries

Real-money gambling, sweepstakes and contests require licensing, geo-restriction and loot-box odds disclosure.

Read the breakdownGuideline 5.4

VPN Apps

Only legal-entity developers may ship VPN apps, and only with the NEVPNManager API. No selling, sharing or using customer data.

Read the breakdownGuideline 5.5

Mobile Device Management

MDM apps are tightly scoped — for organisations managing their own devices, not for spying on consumers or partners.
