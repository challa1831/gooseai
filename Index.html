Guideline 3.1.1 · In-App Purchase

"Your app links to an external paywall." Guideline 3.1.1, in plain English.

12 min read · Updated May 2026 · Most-rejected business-tier guideline

3.1.1 is the most expensive rule in the App Store. It says: if you sell digital content or subscriptions inside your app, you must use Apple's in-app purchase (IAP) — and you can't link out to a website that processes the payment instead. The rule has subtleties that get even careful developers rejected.

What Apple actually says

Standard rejection notice — Guideline 3.1.1 "Your app enables the purchase of digital content for use in the app, but does not use in-app purchase. Apps distributed via the App Store may not provide access to purchased content, subscriptions, or features outside of the app, unless those products were originally purchased outside of the app."

Notice the carve-out at the end — "unless originally purchased outside the app". This is the multi-platform escape hatch (the "reader rule"), and it's the thing most developers get wrong.

The five most common 3.1.1 triggers

  1. External "Subscribe" buttons. Any button, link, or screen that takes the user to a web page where they enter their credit card to subscribe — even if you call it "Manage Subscription" or "Web Plans".
  2. "Sign in to claim premium" flows. If a user can log in inside your app and unlock paid features that weren't purchased through IAP, that's 3.1.1 regardless of where the original purchase happened.
  3. Free trial bypass via web. Offering a 30-day free trial only available by signing up on your website. Apple wants the trial inside the app, using StoreKit subscription introductory pricing.
  4. Promo codes that unlock content. A promo-code field in your app that grants premium without going through IAP is treated as bypass.
  5. Crypto / token payments. Any non-IAP digital-goods payment, including crypto, gift cards purchased on-platform, or third-party processors like Stripe Checkout inside an in-app WebView.

The reader-rule exception (when external links are fine)

If your app is a "reader" app (Spotify, Netflix, Kindle, Adobe — content the user originally purchased somewhere else), Apple permits a single, properly registered external link to your account-management page. You must:

  • Register as a reader app through App Store Connect.
  • Show Apple's mandatory disclosure modal before opening the link.
  • Pass the App Store's Reader entitlement review (separate from app review).

If you haven't filed for the entitlement, the external link will trigger 3.1.1, even if your app is genuinely a reader.

How to fix a 3.1.1 rejection

  1. Find the offending surface. Apple's rejection email usually cites a specific screen number (e.g., "screen 4 of your onboarding"). Open that screen and identify the button, link, or text that mentions an external purchase.
  2. Choose your remedy. Three options: (a) remove the link entirely from iOS builds, (b) replace it with a StoreKit 2 in-app purchase flow, or (c) gate it behind a platform check and only show on macOS/web.
  3. Update screenshots. If your App Store listing screenshots show the offending screen, update them. Reviewers check.
  4. Resubmit with a detailed Resolution Center note. Tell App Review exactly what you changed, where it was, and that the new build (e.g., v3.4.2) routes 100% of subscriptions through IAP. Attach a 10-second screen recording if you can.

Appeal-letter template

Copy & adapt "Hello App Review team, thank you for the detailed feedback on Guideline 3.1.1. We have removed the external subscription link from the iOS onboarding flow on screen 4 and replaced it with our in-app purchase flow using StoreKit 2. The new build (vX.Y.Z) routes 100% of subscription purchases through Apple's IAP system, and we have updated our App Store screenshots to reflect the new flow. Attached: (1) annotated before/after screenshots of screen 4, (2) a 12-second screen recording of the new IAP flow, (3) a list of all openURL() calls remaining in the iOS build, none of which route to paid digital content. Please let us know if you need anything further. Thanks for your time."

What about apps with both subscriptions and one-time purchases?

You can mix IAP-managed subscriptions with non-monetized features. The rule is binary on each item: if the user pays digital money to unlock something, that something must be sold through IAP. Hardware purchases, physical goods, in-person services — none of those trigger 3.1.1.

Bottom line

3.1.1 is solvable in two days for most apps if the offending surface is a single external link. Multi-screen integrations (cross-platform account portals, web onboarding wizards, etc.) usually need a week of engineering plus an entitlement filing. Either way, the reviewer is looking for one thing: a clear, single-line statement of what changed and where. Get that right and the resubmission clears.

Guideline 4.3 · Design Spam

"Your app appears similar to others." Surviving the 4.3 AI-clone dragnet.

10 min read · Updated May 2026 · Most-subjective design rejection

4.3 has tripled in volume since 2024. Apple's reviewers are using it as a catch-all to ground AI-generated content apps, templated affirmations/journals/habit-trackers, and anything that looks like it could have been built by another developer in a weekend. It is the most subjective rejection in the App Store, and there is no formal appeal mechanism — only resubmission with stronger differentiation.

What Apple actually says

Standard rejection notice — Guideline 4.3 "Your app duplicates the content and functionality of other apps in the App Store. Submitting multiple apps with similar content or functionality may be considered a form of spam. To resolve this issue, we encourage you to review your concept and submit a unique app with distinct content and functionality."

The phrase "duplicates the content and functionality" is doing a lot of work here. Apple doesn't tell you which apps yours duplicates, which features count, or how much differentiation is enough. That asymmetry is the whole problem with 4.3.

Why 4.3 rejections have tripled since 2024

  • AI tools collapsed build cost. Cursor and Claude Code make it trivial to ship a "Daily Affirmations" or "AI Bedtime Stories" app in a weekend. Apple sees the resulting submission flood as spam by definition.
  • Templated app studios. Studios shipping 20-30 similar apps under different brand names trigger 4.3 even when each individual app is well-built.
  • Same-content-different-skin clones. Apps that share an ASO playbook (same keywords, similar screenshots, similar paywall) get flagged as a portfolio even when the developers are unrelated.
  • The 4.3(b) sub-clause. "Submitting multiple apps where each one is essentially the same" is being interpreted aggressively against single developers with portfolio strategies.

The five-step 4.3 differentiation checklist

This is what holds up to reviewer scrutiny. Not all five are required, but you need at least three:

  1. Distinct core mechanic. Not just a different theme or color palette — a different user interaction. If your affirmations app is "swipe through cards", at least one differentiated competitor needs to use a different primary interaction (tap-to-reveal, search-based, conversational).
  2. Original content, not LLM-templated. Apple's reviewers can spot LLM-generated affirmations within seconds. If your content is AI-assisted, mix in 30%+ original human-written entries and credit them.
  3. Unique data layer. User-generated content, social graph, scraped community data, integration with a service the app actually depends on (Strava, Apple Health, calendar). Static AI content fails this test.
  4. Distinct monetization. If everyone in your category charges $9.99/month with a 3-day trial, charge differently — even if the price is the same. One-time purchase, lifetime, freemium with a unique premium feature.
  5. Screenshots that don't look like everyone else's. ASO templates from Sensor Tower or similar create same-screenshot syndrome. Custom screenshots with your actual brand colors and flow win.

How to fix a 4.3 rejection

  1. Don't argue. Differentiate. 4.3 appeals almost never win on argument alone. The appeal must point to specific changes you've made, not reasons you should be exempt.
  2. Pick two of the five differentiators above. Implement them in the next build. Ideally one is a UX change (visible to the reviewer immediately) and one is a structural change (content, data, monetization).
  3. Update screenshots and description. Lead with what's new. "Now with [X unique feature]" in the App Store description, and the first screenshot showing the differentiator.
  4. Resubmit with a Resolution Center note. Cite the specific changes. "We have added [unique feature], which is not present in [category]." Don't name competitors directly — describe the category.

Appeal-letter template

Copy & adapt "Hello App Review team, thank you for the feedback on Guideline 4.3. We have made substantive changes to differentiate our app from others in the [category] category. Specifically: (1) we have added [unique mechanic — e.g., a conversational onboarding that adapts based on the user's stated goals], which is not present in other apps in this category; (2) we have replaced the previous templated content library with [N] hours of original human-authored entries, credited per-entry; (3) we have changed our pricing model from monthly subscription to a one-time purchase with optional premium add-ons, differentiating our business model. Attached: before/after screenshots, a 30-second screen recording of the new onboarding, and a public-facing changelog post. We believe the resubmission represents a genuinely distinct offering. Thank you for your time."

Pull the top 20 apps for your primary keyword. If three or more share your color palette, your first-screenshot layout, your subscription price, AND your core mechanic — you're a 4.3 risk regardless of how good your content is. Change at least two before submitting.

What about portfolio studios?

If you're publishing multiple apps under one developer account, the rule of thumb is: each app must read as a distinct product to a reviewer spending 90 seconds on it. Shared backend infrastructure is fine. Shared UI templates are NOT fine. Shared brand voice is fine if each app has a distinct theme. Shared monetization patterns are tolerated up to a point — Apple watches for studios where 5+ apps share the exact subscription paywall design.

Bottom line

4.3 is the rejection most likely to permanently kill a submission strategy if you don't respect it. The fix is structural, not cosmetic. Plan for 1-2 weeks of differentiation work after a 4.3 rejection, not a quick screenshot swap. And if Apple cites 4.3 twice on the same app, take the second one as a signal that the category itself may be saturated to a point where a new submission isn't worth the engineering investment.

Guideline 5.1.1 · ITMS-91053

ITMS-91053 auto-reject: the Privacy Manifest checklist.

11 min read · Updated May 2026 · Apple's most automated rejection code

ITMS-91053 is unique among App Store rejections. It fires before a human reviewer ever opens your build — Apple's automated pre-checks scan your binary for a PrivacyInfo.xcprivacy file declaring each SDK's data-collection practices. If any required-reason API or known SDK is missing a declaration, the build is rejected within minutes of upload, no appeal mechanism.

What Apple actually says

Standard rejection notice — ITMS-91053 "Missing API declaration. Your app's code in the binary submitted uses [API category] but does not contain a corresponding entry in the privacy manifest. Update the privacy manifest of your app to declare your reasons for using this API category and submit a new build for review."

The category is named in the rejection (e.g., "User Defaults", "File Timestamp", "System Boot Time", "Disk Space", "Active Keyboard"). Each category has a fixed list of "approved reasons" you must select from.

The five SDK categories most likely to trigger ITMS-91053

  1. Analytics SDKs. Firebase Analytics, Mixpanel, Amplitude, PostHog. Each has its own published manifest you can copy; missing it is by far the most common ITMS-91053 trigger.
  2. Crash reporters. Sentry, Bugsnag, Crashlytics. These use file-timestamp and active-keyboard APIs that require declaration.
  3. Ad SDKs. AdMob, AppLovin, Unity Ads. Often the worst offenders because they update frequently and break the manifest on minor version bumps.
  4. Subscription managers. RevenueCat, Adapty, Superwall. They've all shipped manifests since mid-2024, but only if you're on a recent SDK version.
  5. UI / native bridges. Some React Native and Flutter bridges, plus older Swift packages that haven't been touched in 2+ years.

How the PrivacyInfo.xcprivacy file works

It's a property list file at the root of your app bundle (and one inside each third-party SDK bundle). It declares four things:

  • NSPrivacyTracking — boolean: does this code track users across apps/sites?
  • NSPrivacyTrackingDomains — list of tracking domains (only required if the above is true).
  • NSPrivacyCollectedDataTypes — array of data types collected and whether each is linked to user identity.
  • NSPrivacyAccessedAPITypes — array of required-reason API categories used, with an approved reason code for each.

The working ITMS-91053 template

This is the template that clears for most apps using common analytics + crash + IAP SDKs. Replace the placeholder reason codes with the ones that apply to your specific usage:

  • NSPrivacyAccessedAPICategoryUserDefaults → reason CA92.1 (storing app preferences)
  • NSPrivacyAccessedAPICategoryFileTimestamp → reason C617.1 (display to user)
  • NSPrivacyAccessedAPICategorySystemBootTime → reason 35F9.1 (measure time elapsed in-app)
  • NSPrivacyAccessedAPICategoryDiskSpace → reason 85F4.1 (write data only when sufficient space)

The full list of approved reason codes is in Apple's developer docs. Picking the wrong one is treated as "missing declaration" and re-fires ITMS-91053.

How to fix an ITMS-91053 rejection

  1. Read the rejection email carefully. It names the exact API category. Don't try to declare everything — declare what's missing.
  2. Identify which SDK triggered it. Apple sometimes names the SDK; if not, scan your Pods or Package.resolved for SDKs known to use that API category.
  3. Update the SDK to a recent version. Most major SDKs ship their own manifest. Updating the SDK often solves the rejection without you touching PrivacyInfo.xcprivacy at all.
  4. Add or extend your app's manifest. If the SDK doesn't ship one, you add the declaration in your app's manifest with the appropriate reason code.
  5. Re-archive and re-submit. ITMS-91053 fires on upload, so you'll know within an hour whether the fix worked. There's no human review delay.

Appeal-letter template

You don't typically appeal ITMS-91053 — you re-upload. But if Apple repeatedly rejects despite a correct manifest, the Resolution Center note should read:

Copy & adapt "Hello App Review team, this build (vX.Y.Z) includes an updated PrivacyInfo.xcprivacy manifest declaring the [API category] reason code [CODE]. The declaration was added in response to your prior rejection email dated [date]. The relevant SDK is [SDK name], version [X.Y.Z], which uses this API for [specific feature — e.g., 'storing user-selected preferences for display order']. We have attached a screenshot of the manifest entry. Please let us know if any additional declaration is needed."

The trickiest case: indirect dependencies

If you use a wrapper SDK (e.g., Firebase) that bundles other SDKs (Crashlytics, Analytics, Auth), the manifest needs to cover all of them. Firebase 11.x ships a proper combined manifest; Firebase 10.x and below do not. Upgrading to 11.x is the single highest-leverage fix for studios on older versions.
